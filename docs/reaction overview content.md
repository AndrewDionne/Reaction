9A — Variation and inheritance
Sub‑units
Variation: Environmental and inherited variation within species, continuous vs discontinuous variation and classification into species.
DNA and inheritance: Chromosomes, genes and DNA; gametes, fertilisation and zygotes; predicting the inheritance of characteristics.
Adaptation and natural selection: How adaptations to habitats arise and how natural selection leads to evolution.
Extinction and biodiversity: Causes of endangerment/extinction and methods for conserving biodiversity.
Must know vocabulary

These terms and their meanings come from the 9A word sheets.

Monsters and myth (9Aa): genus, species, variation, journal, scientific paper.
Environmental variation (9Aa): abiotic factor, biotic factor, characteristic, classification, continuous data, continuous variation, discontinuous data, discontinuous variation, environment, environmental factor, environmental variation, physical environmental factor, resource.
Inherited variation (9Ab): fertilisation, fuse, gamete, gene, genetic information, inherit, inherited variation, normal distribution, nucleus, offspring, parent, sexual reproduction, zygote.
Probability (9Ab WS): correlation, estimate, probability, sample.
DNA and cell division (9Ac): cell division, chromosome, DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), gene (repeated here to emphasise), sex chromosome.
Genes, extinction and adaptation (9Ad): adaptation, biodiversity, competition, ecosystem, endangered, extinct, food web, gene bank, native.
Natural selection (9Ae): evolution, natural selection.
Must understand
Explain the difference between environmental and inherited variation, giving examples of each.
Describe how abiotic and biotic factors influence variation and define an ecosystem as the combination of organisms and physical factors in a habitat.
Explain that genes, organised on chromosomes, are sections of DNA carrying instructions for characteristics.
Describe how gametes (sex cells) fuse during fertilisation to form a zygote, resulting in offspring that inherit chromosomes from both parents.
Recognise that probabilities can be used to predict the likelihood of inherited characteristics.
Define an adaptation and describe examples of how animals and plants are adapted to their environments.
Explain natural selection as the process where individuals with advantageous inherited variations are more likely to survive and reproduce, leading to evolution.
List reasons why species become endangered or extinct (changes in physical conditions, competition, disease, human activities) and summarise ways to conserve biodiversity (nature reserves, breeding programmes, hunting bans, gene banks).
Must be able to identify
Identify whether a trait in a data set is continuous or discontinuous (e.g. height vs blood group) and recognise a normal distribution.
Distinguish between environmental and inherited causes of variation when given examples.
Label a diagram showing the hierarchy cell → nucleus → chromosome → DNA → gene.
Identify graphs showing a normal distribution of variation in a characteristic (bell‑shaped curve).
Recognise biotic and abiotic factors in descriptions of ecosystems.
Must memorise

There are no equations in this unit, but students should memorise:

the hierarchical order of genetic material (cell → nucleus → chromosome → DNA → gene),
the definitions of key terms (gene, adaptation, species, etc.),
factors affecting biodiversity and conservation methods.
9B — Plant growth and photosynthesis
Sub‑units
Photosynthesis: Word and symbol equations for photosynthesis and the role of chlorophyll.
Water uptake and minerals: How roots absorb water and dissolved minerals via root hair cells and xylem.
Carbon dioxide and gas exchange: How CO₂ enters leaves through stomata and factors that influence its diffusion.
Light and chloroplasts: The need for light and chlorophyll; leaf adaptations such as large surface area and palisade cells.
Respiration and glucose: Word and symbol equations for aerobic respiration; uses of glucose for energy, storage and growth.
Farming and breeding: Impacts of modern farming, sustainable development, selective breeding and cross‑breeding.
Must know vocabulary

The vocabulary below comes from the 9B word sheets.

On a farm/Reactions in plants (9Ba): criteria, organic farming, aerobic respiration, chlorophyll, chloroplast, glucose, limiting factor, phloem tissue/vessel, photosynthesis, product, rate, raw material, reactant, xylem tissue/vessel.
Plant adaptations (9Bb): adaptation, cuticle, diffusion, epidermis cell, gas exchange, guard cell, mineral/mineral salt, palisade cell, root hair cell, stoma/stomata, surface area, wilting, xylem tissue, phloem tissue.
Plant products (9Bc): amino acid, cellulose, fat, germination, lipid, nitrate, oil, polymer, starch.
Competition and farming practices (9Bd): competition, cross‑breeding, decomposer, fungicide, herbicide, insecticide, pest, pesticide, selective breeding, selective (herbicide), variety, weedkiller, yield.
Protecting wild plants and farming problems (9Bd): endemic (native), algae, carbon cycle, eutrophication, global warming, persistent (pollutants), sustainable development.
Bias and validity (9Bd): bias, random, systematic error, valid.
Must understand
Recall and interpret the word and symbol equations for photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen.
Explain why light energy and chlorophyll are essential for photosynthesis.
Describe how roots absorb water from soil and transport it through xylem vessels; include adaptations such as branching and root hair cells.
Explain why plants need mineral salts (e.g. nitrates for proteins, magnesium for chlorophyll) and how water prevents wilting.
Describe how carbon dioxide diffuses into leaves through stomata and how leaf structure (thin leaves) aids diffusion; explain why increasing CO₂ concentration speeds up photosynthesis.
Explain leaf adaptations for capturing light, including large surface area, arrangement to avoid shading and the role of palisade cells packed with chloroplasts.
Recall and interpret the word and symbol equations for aerobic respiration and recognise that all living cells respire.
Describe the three main uses of glucose: respiration, storage as starch or other substances and synthesis of new materials like cellulose, lipids and proteins.
Explain how modern farming practices (clearing land, using pesticides, fertilisers, monocultures) increase crop yields but damage habitats, food webs and biodiversity.
Describe selective breeding and cross‑breeding for desirable plant characteristics.
Must be able to identify
Identify reactants and products in the photosynthesis and respiration equations.
Recognise adaptations of leaves and roots in photographs or diagrams (e.g. root hair cells, palisade cell layers, stomata).
Identify the direction of water transport in xylem versus nutrient transport in phloem.
Distinguish sustainable farming practices from unsustainable ones in case studies (e.g. organic farming vs monocultures).
Identify examples of selective breeding and cross‑breeding from descriptions.
Must memorise
Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (balanced symbol equation: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂).
Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (balanced symbol equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy).
Limiting factors: recognise that light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature can limit the rate of photosynthesis.
9E — Materials and their impact
Sub‑units
Problems with making and using materials: Pollution from burning fossil fuels (acid rain, greenhouse effect, soot) and accumulation of toxic waste and non‑biodegradable polymer waste.
Solutions and recycling: Strategies to reduce pollution (desulfurisation, renewable energy, waste control, biodegradable polymers) and the benefits of recycling.
Recycling processes: How common materials (metals, glass, polymers, paper, concrete) are recycled.
Endothermic and exothermic reactions: Distinction between reactions that transfer energy to the surroundings and those that absorb energy.
Chemical equations: Using state symbols in equations; example of thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate.
Peer review: The process by which scientific papers are reviewed before publication.
Must know vocabulary

Terms drawn from the 9E word sheets.

About ceramics (9Ea): bond, brittle, ceramic, clay, crystals, heat resistant, insulator, lattice structure, sand, unreactive.
Polymers (9Eb): addition polymer, addition polymerisation, crude oil, elastic, endothermic, exothermic, monomer, plastic, polymer, raw material, synthetic polymer, vulcanisation.
Peer review (9Eb – peer review): aim, conclusion, evaluation, hypothesis, journal, method, peer review, references, results, scientific paper.
Composite materials (9Ec): aggregate, cement, composite material, concrete, fibre, state symbols, thermal decomposition.
Problems with materials (9Ed): acid rain, biodegradable, carbon capture technology, climate change, greenhouse effect, impurity, incomplete combustion, non‑biodegradable.
Recycling materials (9Ee): finite, landfill site, metal, recycling (metals, glass, polymers, paper, concrete).
Must understand
Describe how burning fossil fuels releases pollutants (sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide and soot) and explain their effects: acid rain, the greenhouse effect and air pollution.
Explain how toxic substances released as waste can accumulate in food chains, causing biomagnification, and how non‑biodegradable polymers persist in the environment.
Outline solutions to these problems, including removing sulfur from fuels, switching to renewable energy sources, controlling hazardous waste and using biodegradable polymers.
Summarise the benefits of recycling in reducing landfill, saving energy, reducing pollution and conserving raw materials.
Describe how materials are recycled: metals and glass are melted and reformed; polymers are sorted by recycle labels; paper is pulped after removing ink; concrete is crushed and graded.
Distinguish between exothermic and endothermic reactions, linking them to temperature changes in the surroundings.
Recognise state symbols in chemical equations and interpret the example of thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate.
Describe how the peer‑review process ensures that scientific investigations are checked and repeated before publication.
Must be able to identify
Identify examples of exothermic and endothermic reactions from descriptions or temperature‑change graphs.
Recognise recycling symbols and match them to the correct material type (1 = PET, 2 = HDPE, etc.).
Distinguish between renewable and non‑renewable energy sources in the context of materials manufacture.
Identify the key steps in the peer‑review process when given a flow chart or description.
Must memorise
Thermal decomposition of limestone: CaCO₃ (s) → CaO (s) + CO₂ (g). Students should remember the state symbols and be able to balance similar equations.
Examples of polymers: know that addition polymerisation joins monomer molecules without releasing any by‑products (e.g. formation of poly(ethene)).
9F — Reactivity and reactions
Sub‑units
Reactions and equations: Conservation of atoms in physical and chemical changes and interpreting word and symbol equations.
Gas pressure: How changing temperature, particle number or container volume affects pressure.
Reactivity series: Ordering metals by reactivity and predicting their reactions with water, acids and oxygen.
Atomic structure and the periodic table: Composition of atoms, definitions of atomic and mass numbers, and trends in groups and periods.
Hydrocarbons and combustion: Composition of hydrocarbons and their complete combustion products.
Reaction rates: Factors that affect the rate of chemical reactions and energy changes.
Displacement and redox reactions: More reactive metals displacing less reactive metals, oxidation and reduction.
Metal extraction: Sources of metals, extraction by heating with carbon versus electrolysis, and definitions of oxidation and reduction.
Must know vocabulary

These terms come from the 9F word sheets.

Demolition (9Fa): explosive, implosion, pressure.
Types of explosion: chemical reaction, combustion, flammable, particle model, particle theory, physical change.
Reactivity (9Fb): atomic number, electron, group, mass number, neutron, nucleus, oxidation, period, periodic table, proton, proton number, reactivity series, sub‑atomic particle.
Energy and reactions (9Fc): endothermic, exothermic, hydrocarbon, neutralisation, oxidising agent, rate, rate of reaction.
Displacement and redox (9Fd): displacement reaction, thermite reaction.
Extracting metals (9Fe): mineral/mineral salt, native state, ore, redox reaction, reduced, reducing agent.
Must understand
Explain that in both physical changes and chemical reactions the number of atoms stays the same, so there is no overall change in mass.
Interpret word and symbol equations and identify the meaning of coefficients (number of molecules) and subscripts (number of atoms in a molecule); know the state symbols (g, l, s, aq).
Describe gas pressure as the result of particles colliding with container walls and explain how pressure increases with temperature, number of particles and decreasing volume.
Use the reactivity series to predict which metals will react with water, dilute acids or oxygen and what products will form.
Summarise the atomic structure of elements, defining protons, neutrons and electrons, and explain the meaning of atomic number and mass number.
Explain that hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen and that complete combustion forms carbon dioxide and water.
Describe factors that increase the rate of reaction—temperature, concentration, surface area and the presence of an oxidising agent—and distinguish between exothermic and endothermic energy changes.
Explain displacement reactions as situations where a more reactive metal replaces a less reactive metal in a compound and define oxidation as gain of oxygen and reduction as loss of oxygen.
Describe where metals are found (ores) and outline methods of extracting metals: heating with carbon for metals below zinc in the reactivity series and electrolysis for metals above zinc; explain the role of reducing agents.
Must be able to identify
Identify balanced and unbalanced chemical equations; state the number of atoms and molecules in a given reaction.
Recognise factors that will increase or decrease gas pressure in everyday examples (e.g. inflating a balloon, heating a sealed container).
Identify positions of metals in the reactivity series and predict whether they will react with water, dilute acids or oxygen.
Label the parts of an atom and identify an element’s position in the periodic table using its atomic number.
Identify whether an energy‑profile diagram represents an exothermic or endothermic reaction.
Predict the products of displacement reactions and extraction methods based on a metal’s reactivity.
Must memorise
Balanced reaction example: 2 H₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2 H₂O (l).
Hydrocarbon combustion: hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
Gas pressure relationships: increasing temperature or particle number, or decreasing volume, increases pressure.
Percentage change formula: percentage change = (actual change ÷ original amount) × 100.
9I — Forces, motion and machines
Sub‑units
Forces and motion: How forces change an object’s motion and why constant speed requires balanced forces.
Falling and terminal velocity: How air resistance increases until it balances weight, leading to terminal velocity.
Speed and relative speed: Measuring speed, unit conversions and calculating mean and relative speed.
Distance–time and speed–time graphs: Using gradients to determine speed and interpreting features of graphs.
Gradients and calculations: Calculating speed from graph gradients.
Levers and moments: Definitions of pivot, moments, force multipliers and distance multipliers; formula for moments.
Balance and equilibrium: When anticlockwise and clockwise moments are equal.
Simple machines and work: Examples of simple machines; definition of work and how to calculate it.
Must know vocabulary

Forces and movement (9Ia): accelerate, air resistance, balanced force, drag, fluid, friction, resultant force, terminal velocity, unbalanced force, upthrust, water resistance, weight.
Energy for movement (9Ib): chemical energy, deform, dissipate, efficiency, elastic potential energy (strain energy), electricity, fossil fuel, gravitational potential energy, heating, internal energy (thermal energy), kinetic energy, law of conservation of energy, light, non‑renewable resource, nuclear energy, renewable resource, solar energy, sound waves, strain energy, thermal energy.

Must understand
Explain how forces affect motion—forces can speed up, slow down or change direction; constant speed requires forces to be balanced.
Describe why a falling object reaches terminal velocity when air resistance balances weight.
Calculate speed by measuring distance and time, and convert between m/s and km/h.
Define mean (average) speed and relative speed and compute them in context.
Interpret distance–time and speed–time graphs: steepness indicates speed; horizontal lines on speed–time graphs show constant speed; slopes show acceleration or deceleration.
Calculate the gradient of a line on a distance–time graph to determine speed.
Explain how levers use pivots to multiply force or distance and define a moment; state the formula: moment = force × perpendicular distance.
Recognise that objects are in equilibrium when clockwise and anticlockwise moments are equal and use this principle to solve problems.
Describe how simple machines (levers, ramps, pulleys) reduce the force needed by increasing distance; explain that the total work done (energy transferred) is the same.
Define work as force × distance moved in the direction of the force and use it to calculate energy transfers.
Must be able to identify
Identify scenarios where forces are balanced vs unbalanced.
Recognise diagrams of objects reaching terminal velocity (e.g. skydiver with forces balanced) and explain the forces.
Read a distance–time or speed–time graph and describe the motion of the object.
Identify the pivot, effort and load on diagrams of levers and predict which way the lever will turn; determine whether a lever is a force multiplier or a distance multiplier.
Match descriptions of situations to the correct work calculation and identify examples of energy conversion (e.g. gravitational potential to kinetic).
Must memorise
Speed formula: speed = distance ÷ time.
Moment formula: moment = force × perpendicular distance.
Work formula: work = force × distance moved in the direction of the force.
Weight: weight = mass × gravitational field strength (≈ 10 N/kg).
9J — Electricity and magnetism
Sub‑units
Force fields: Definition of force fields, magnetic fields, gravitational fields and the formula for weight.
Static electricity: Charges in atoms; transfer of electrons by rubbing; attraction and repulsion; electric fields.
Electric circuits: Series and parallel circuits; definition and measurement of current and voltage.
Resistance: What resistance means, how it affects current and how material, length and thickness influence it; Ohm’s law.
Electromagnets: Magnetic field around a current‑carrying wire; structure of an electromagnet and how to make it stronger.
Motors and the motor effect: A current‑carrying wire in a magnetic field experiences a force; structure of an electric motor; factors affecting motor speed.
Must know vocabulary

These terms are taken from the 9J word sheets.

Force fields (9Ja): force field, gravitational field, gravitational field strength, gravitational potential energy, magnetic field, mass, non‑contact force, weight.
Static electricity (9Jb): electric field, electron, negative charge, neutron, nucleus, positive charge, proton, static electricity.
Current electricity (9Jc): ammeter, ampere (A), electric current, parallel circuit, series circuit, volt (V), voltage, voltmeter.
Resistance (9Jd): ohm (Ω), resistance, resistor.
Rounding numbers (9Jd WS): decimal place, significant figure.
Electromagnets and motors (9Je): electric motor, electromagnet, motor effect, relay.
Must understand
Define a force field and describe magnetic and gravitational fields; recall that weight = mass × gravitational field strength and that gravitational potential energy increases with mass and height.
Explain static electricity as the transfer of electrons between insulating materials by friction; understand why an object gaining electrons becomes negatively charged and one losing electrons becomes positively charged.
Describe the attraction and repulsion between charged objects and define an electric field as the space around a charged object where other charges feel a force.
Distinguish between series and parallel circuits; define current as a flow of electrons and explain how to measure current (ammeter in series) and voltage (voltmeter in parallel).
Explain resistance as a measure of how difficult it is for current to flow; describe how increasing voltage increases current and increasing resistance decreases current; list factors affecting the resistance of a wire (material, length, thickness).
State Ohm’s law (voltage = current × resistance) and rearrange it to calculate resistance or current.
Describe how an electromagnet is made from a coil of wire carrying current; compare its magnetic field to that of a bar magnet and explain how increasing the number of coils, increasing the current or adding an iron core makes it stronger.
Explain the motor effect: a wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force; describe how an electric motor uses this effect and how increasing the current, magnetic field strength or number of coil turns increases motor speed.
Must be able to identify
Identify electric fields and magnetic fields in diagrams and determine the direction of field lines (arrows show direction a positive charge would move).
Recognise series versus parallel circuit diagrams and correctly place ammeters and voltmeters.
Identify factors that will increase or decrease the resistance of a wire in an experiment.
Label a simple electromagnet and identify ways to increase its strength.
Analyse a diagram of an electric motor and identify the forces acting on the coil (left hand rule) and how changing current or field strength affects rotation.
Must memorise
Weight: weight = mass × gravitational field strength (≈ 10 N/kg).
Ohm’s law: voltage = current × resistance.
Current from voltage and resistance: current = voltage ÷ resistance.
Gravitational potential energy: recognise that raising an object of mass m by height h increases its gravitational potential energy by m × g × h.
